What You Are Building, and Why It Is DefensibleMost “regime detection” student projects fit a 3-state Hidden Markov Model (HMM) to daily returns and call it done. The published literature is explicit that this standard approach has a structural weakness: it assumes the probability of switching regimes is constant on every trading day (a time-homogeneous Markov chain), and as a result it can only confirm a regime change after it has already happened — it cannot anticipate one. Separately, the standard approach treats every trading day as informationally identical, even though NSE publishes a public, dated calendar of scheduled corporate events (results, board meetings, AGMs) that are known triggers of regime shifts.This project closes that specific, citable gap: you build a non-homogeneous HMM whose transition probabilities are allowed to change as a function of “days to/from the nearest scheduled corporate event.” This requires you to partially re-derive the forward–backward and Viterbi algorithms, which assume homogeneity in their standard textbook form — this is the part no off-the-shelf library or AI tool does for you, because it requires you to understand the EM derivation well enough to modify it correctly and defend why your modification is mathematically valid.You then hand-verify a small set of cases yourself: for roughly 80–100 real corporate events, you read the actual filing (not just the price move) and judge whether the regime shift the model detected was actually justified by the disclosed content. This is the second moat — a scraper or LLM can summarize a filing, but it cannot make the financial judgment call of “was this regime shift warranted,” because that requires holding the model's output, the raw filing, and your own financial reasoning together at once.The two things that make this yours, not AI's1. The math: a non-homogeneous HMM is a real, derivable extension of your MSc stochastic processes coursework. You should be able to write the modified forward–backward recursion on a whiteboard and explain why the homogeneous-chain assumption breaks near scheduled events.2. The hand-verified set: 80–100 events where you personally read the filing and labeled whether the regime call was fundamentally justified. This is the part that cannot be faked, scraped, or auto-labeled — and it is what you can speak to in detail in an interview.2. Where This Plan Could Slip — and the FallbackTwo-week timelines for a project with a real math derivation are tight. Build in these fallbacks now so a delay on one day doesn't sink the whole plan.If Day 4–5 (the derivation/implementation) takes longer than planned: simplify to exactly two transition matrices (event-window vs. not), which is already the plan's default — do not attempt a continuous function of days-to-event in two weeks.If NSE's event calendar data is harder to bulk-pull than expected: fall back to manually compiling events for a smaller shortlist of 8–10 stocks (still enough for 80–100 verified events if you pick stocks with frequent corporate actions).If hand-verification (Days 8–9) is slower than expected: prioritize quality and consistency over hitting exactly 100 — 60–70 carefully verified events with a clear rubric is more defensible than 150 rushed ones.If the event-conditioned model does not clearly beat the baseline: report this honestly as the finding. “I built and validated a mathematically motivated extension and found it improves precision near events but not overall Sharpe” is a legitimate, interview-defensible result — do not manipulate the backtest to force a win.
