Before any math, you need to know what every word in that title means. Let's go term by term, building outward from the absolute basics.1.1  What is a stock, and what is “NSE”?A stock (also called a share or equity) is a small ownership slice of a company. If a company is divided into 1,000,000 shares and you own 1, you own one-millionth of that company. Companies sell shares to raise money; in exchange, owners get a claim on future profits and a vote in some company decisions.Stocks are bought and sold on a stock exchange — a regulated marketplace that matches buyers and sellers. NSE = National Stock Exchange of India, the largest stock exchange in India (the other major one is BSE, the Bombay Stock Exchange). When you hear “NSE-listed stock,” it just means a company whose shares trade on this exchange.NIFTY50 is not a single stock — it's an index: a single number computed from the prices of the 50 largest, most liquid companies on NSE, weighted by their size. It's used as a proxy for “how is the Indian stock market doing overall today,” the way you might use a class average to summarize 50 individual test scores. Your project uses NIFTY50 as the main thing whose “regime” you're trying to detect, plus 15–20 individual stocks.Large-cap = a company with a large total market value (market capitalization = share price × number of shares). Large-cap stocks are generally more stable and more heavily traded (“liquid,” meaning easy to buy/sell without moving the price much) than small companies.1.2  Price, returns, and why we use “log returns” instead of raw pricesIf a stock's price moves from ₹100 to ₹105, that's a gain of ₹5, or 5% return. We almost never analyze raw prices directly in this kind of project — we analyze returns, for a simple, important reason:A stock that goes ₹10 → ₹15 has the same percentage move (50% up) as one that goes ₹1000 → ₹1500, even though the raw price change (₹5 vs ₹500) looks wildly different. Returns put every stock on the same comparable scale, regardless of its price level.There are two ways to compute returns:Simple return: (Pₜ − Pₜ₋₁) / Pₜ₋₁ — today's price minus yesterday's, divided by yesterday's.Log return: ln(Pₜ / Pₜ₋₁) — the natural logarithm of today's price divided by yesterday's.Your project explicitly asks for log returns. Why log, not simple? Three mathematical reasons that matter a lot once we get into the HMM:Additivity over time. If you want the 5-day return from a sequence of 1-day returns, log returns simply add up: ln(P₅/P₀) = ln(P₁/P₀) + ln(P₂/P₁) + ... + ln(P₅/P₄). Simple returns don't add this cleanly — you'd have to multiply (1+r) terms together. Since your whole model is a sequence of daily steps, additive returns make every downstream calculation (means, variances, sums) much cleaner.Symmetry. A simple return of +50% followed by −50% does not bring you back to where you started (it leaves you down 25%: 100 → 150 → 75). Log returns are symmetric: a log return of +x followed by −x exactly cancels. This symmetry matters because the HMM models returns using a Normal (Gaussian) distribution, which is itself symmetric — log returns are a much better fit to that assumption than simple returns are.Closer to Normally distributed. Stock prices themselves are best thought of as evolving multiplicatively (a few % up or down each day, compounding), not additively. Taking the log of a multiplicative process turns it into something closer to an additive, bell-curve-shaped (Normal) process — exactly the assumption the HMM's emission model needs (more on “emission” soon).So: log return on day t = ln(closing price on day t / closing price on day t−1). This is the core number your entire model is built on.1.3  OHLCV data“Pull 5 years of daily OHLCV data” — OHLCV is just an acronym for the five numbers recorded for every stock, every trading day:Open — price at the start of the trading sessionHigh — highest price reached during the dayLow — lowest price reached during the dayClose — price at the end of the session (this is the number most analysis uses — it's the “settled” price for the day)Volume — total number of shares traded that dayYour project mainly uses the Close price (to compute log returns) and sometimes Volume/High/Low for sanity checks.1.4  VolatilityVolatility is a measure of how much a stock's returns swing around — how “jumpy” or “calm” the stock has been. Mathematically, it's usually the standard deviation of returns over some window of time.Low volatility = returns cluster tightly around their average (calm market).High volatility = returns are scattered widely, with big up and down days (turbulent market).Your project computes rolling 10-day realized volatility — meaning: for each day, look back at the last 10 days of returns, and compute their standard deviation. “Rolling” means this window slides forward one day at a time, so you get a volatility number for every single day, not just one number for the whole 5 years. This turns volatility into a second time series (alongside returns) that can also feed into the model.Why does volatility matter for “regimes”? Because market regimes are often distinguished as much by how turbulent things are as by whether prices are going up or down. A “crash” regime isn't just “prices fall” — it's “prices fall and swing wildly.” A “calm bull market” regime is “prices rise steadily, with small day-to-day moves.” Volatility is one of the clearest fingerprints separating one regime from another.1.5  What is a “market regime”?This is the central concept of the whole project, so let's be precise.A regime is a hidden, persistent state of the market that shapes how returns behave for a stretch of time, until something causes the market to switch into a different regime. The intuition: financial markets don't behave the same way every single day in a totally independent, random fashion. Instead, they seem to move through phases:A calm/bull regime: small positive average returns, low volatility, gentle upward drift. Think of a normal “everything is fine” period.A volatile/bear regime: negative average returns, high volatility, sharp swings. Think of a crash or crisis period — 2020 COVID crash is the example your project plan literally suggests checking for.A sideways/neutral regime: returns hover near zero, moderate volatility — no strong trend either way.Crucially: you never directly observe which regime the market is in. You only observe the outputs of being in that regime — the actual daily returns and volatility numbers. The regime itself is a hidden cause you have to infer statistically. This is precisely why the tool for this job is called a Hidden Markov Model — “hidden” refers to the regime being unobservable, only inferable from what you can see (the returns).1.6  Corporate events and the NSE Event CalendarA corporate event here means a scheduled, publicly announced in advance, company-specific occurrence that can move that company's stock price:Results / Earnings announcements — every public company reports its financial performance (revenue, profit, etc.) on a quarterly basis, on a pre-announced date. These are famous for causing big single-day price moves, because the market is comparing actual results to what it expected.Board meetings — meetings where company directors decide things (e.g., approve a dividend, approve a big investment, approve results before they're announced).AGM (Annual General Meeting) — yearly meeting where shareholders vote on company matters and management gives updates.NSE publishes a public Corporate Filings Event Calendar — a dated schedule of these events for every listed company, available for anyone to look up in advance. This is the critical fact your whole project hinges on: you know exact future dates when a stock is “at risk” of a regime-changing event, before it happens. This is fundamentally different information from “did volatility spike yesterday” — it's forward-looking, calendar-based knowledge.1.7  Why scheduled events matter for regime detection — the project's core financial insightHere is the actual intellectual claim your project is testing, stated in plain English:Markets don't switch regimes uniformly at random on any given day. They are more likely to switch regimes on or around days when new, material information is scheduled to be revealed (like an earnings report) — because that's when genuinely new facts about a company hit the market, as opposed to ordinary day-to-day noise trading.A standard regime model treats every day as equally likely to see a regime switch — Tuesday is no different from “the day before quarterly earnings are announced.” But intuitively, that's wrong: the days immediately surrounding a results announcement are exactly when you'd expect volatility to spike and a regime shift to actually occur, because that's when uncertainty about the company gets resolved (or made worse) by real new information.Your project's mathematical contribution is to bake this intuition into the model itself — to let the probability of switching regimes depend on whether you're near a scheduled event or not, rather than assuming it's constant every day. That's the “event-conditioned” and “non-homogeneous” part of the title, which Part 3 will make fully precise.1.8  Backtesting, Sharpe ratio, drawdown, win rate — the “did this actually work” toolkitOnce you've built a model that calls “regime A” or “regime B” for each day, you need to ask: is this information actually useful? The standard way to test that in finance is backtesting: simulate a simple trading strategy using only information your model would have had at the time (never future information — that would be cheating / “look-ahead bias”), and see how that strategy would have performed historically.Your project's backtest is simple: “a long/avoid strategy gated on regime state” — meaning, e.g., “if the model says we're in the calm/bull regime, hold the stock; if it says we're in the crash regime, step aside.” Then you measure:Sharpe ratio: a single number measuring return earned per unit of risk taken. Roughly: Sharpe = (average return − risk-free rate) / standard deviation of returns. Higher Sharpe = better risk-adjusted performance. A strategy that returns 20% with wild swings can have a worse Sharpe ratio than one that returns 10% smoothly — Sharpe rewards consistency, not just raw gains.Max drawdown: the largest peak-to-trough decline your strategy ever experienced — i.e., if you started with ₹100 and at some point it dropped (from a prior peak) all the way down to ₹70 before recovering, your max drawdown is 30%. This measures the worst pain an investor following this strategy would have endured.Win rate: the fraction of trades (or trading periods) that were profitable.Buy-and-hold benchmark: the simplest possible “strategy” — just buy the stock/index once and hold it the whole time, no trading at all. Your model-driven strategy needs to be compared against this baseline; if you can't beat simple buy-and-hold, that's an important (and acceptable, per your plan) honest finding, not a failure.1.9  SHAP and XGBoost (Day 11) — a quick previewThese appear later in your plan as an additional layer on top of the regime model, so a brief preview is enough for now (Part 5 covers it properly):XGBoost is a popular, powerful general-purpose machine learning algorithm for prediction tasks (it builds many small decision trees and combines them). Here it's used to predict a buy/hold/avoid signal from a handful of features (the regime, momentum, volatility ratio, distance to nearest event).SHAP is a technique to explain why a machine learning model made a particular prediction — for each individual prediction, it tells you how much each input feature pushed the prediction up or down. Used here to confirm that “distance to event” is actually an influential feature near scheduled events, giving evidence (beyond the HMM math itself) that event-timing matters.Checkpoint — Part 1✓  What a log return is and why log (not simple) returns are used.✓  What “regime” means in this project, and why it's “hidden.”✓  What the NSE Event Calendar gives you that ordinary price data doesn't.✓  The core hypothesis the whole project tests (events → higher chance of regime switch nearby).✓  What Sharpe ratio, max drawdown, and buy-and-hold mean and why they matter for judging the model.Part 2 — Markov Chains: The Foundation Before “Hidden”You said you know decent math, so this part will move a bit faster on probability notation, but won't assume you've seen Markov chains or HMMs before.2.1  Random variables and stochastic processes, brieflyA random variable is a quantity whose value isn't fixed but follows some probability distribution. A stochastic process is just a sequence (or family) of random variables indexed by time: X₁, X₂, X₃, .... Daily stock returns are a stochastic process — each day's return is a random variable, and the whole sequence over 5 years is the process.2.2  What is a Markov chain?A Markov chain is a stochastic process over a discrete set of states (e.g., “Regime A,” “Regime B,” “Regime C”) with one defining property, called the Markov property:The probability of moving to the next state depends only on the current state — not on the entire history of how you got there.Formally, if Sₜ denotes the state at time t:P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i, S_{t-2}, ..., S_1)  =  P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i)In words: knowing the state yesterday is all you need to predict tomorrow's state probabilities. Knowing the state two days ago, or ten years ago, gives you no extra information once you already know yesterday's state. This is sometimes called “memorylessness.”Why is this a reasonable simplification for market regimes? The idea is: if the market is currently calm, the chance it stays calm tomorrow (versus flipping to a crash) is some fixed probability that doesn't depend on whether it was calm for 3 days or 300 days before that. It's a simplifying assumption — real markets surely have more memory than this — but it's tractable and a well-established, citable starting point. (Indeed, the central improvement in your project is to relax exactly this assumption — see Part 2.4.)2.3  The transition matrixIf there are N possible states, the chain's behavior is completely summarized by an N × N transition matrix, usually called A:A[i][j] = P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i)That is, entry (i, j) is the probability of moving from state i to state j in one time step. For a 3-state model (your project's baseline: 3 regimes), A looks like:            To: Regime1  Regime2  Regime3From Regime1:    0.95     0.03     0.02From Regime2:    0.05     0.90     0.05From Regime3:    0.02     0.08     0.90Read row by row: “if I'm in Regime 1 today, there's a 95% chance I'm still in Regime 1 tomorrow, 3% chance I move to Regime 2, 2% chance I move to Regime 3.”Key constraint: every row must sum to 1. This is because, given you're in some state today, you must end up in some state tomorrow (possibly the same one) — the probabilities of all possible “next steps” must account for 100% of what could happen.Notice the diagonal entries (0.95, 0.90, 0.90) are large. This reflects an important real-world property of market regimes: they're persistent — if you're in a calm regime today, you're very likely still in a calm regime tomorrow, not because nothing ever changes, but because regimes, by definition, last for stretches of time (weeks or months), not single days. This persistence is what makes regimes a meaningful, useful concept rather than just noise.2.4  Time-homogeneous vs. non-homogeneous Markov chains — the key distinction for your entire projectThis is the single most important conceptual fork in the whole project, so sit with it carefully.Time-homogeneous Markov chain: the transition matrix A is the same matrix on every single day, for the entire dataset. The probability of switching from Regime 1 to Regime 2 is, say, always exactly 3%, whether it's a random Tuesday in March or the day before a company reports quarterly earnings.A is fixed:  same A used for t = 1, 2, 3, ..., TNon-homogeneous (a.k.a. time-inhomogeneous) Markov chain: the transition matrix is allowed to change depending on time (or, more precisely in your project, depending on some external condition at each time step):A_t can differ across t:  A_1, A_2, ..., A_T  (not all identical)In a fully general non-homogeneous chain, you could in principle have a different transition matrix for every single day — but that's wildly impractical to estimate (you'd need a huge amount of data per day to pin down each matrix reliably, and 5 years of data isn't enough). Your project plan explicitly recognizes this and proposes the simplest tractable version: instead of a different matrix for every day, you define just two transition matrices, selected by a simple rule:A_event-window  ->  used on days within +/-5 trading days of a scheduled corporate eventA_normal        ->  used on all other daysSo technically, this is a Markov chain whose transition matrix is a function of an external “event-window” indicator variable — on each day, you check “am I within 5 trading days of an event for this stock?” (yes/no), and you plug in the matching transition matrix. This is mathematically a clean, well-defined extension class (sometimes the literature calls this a “regime-switching model with covariate-dependent transition probabilities,” or, in your project's words, “a regime-switching model with a regime-dependent transition structure” — note the layered terminology: the Markov chain's states are the regimes, and separately the transition matrix itself switches based on the event-window covariate).Why this is “mathematically valid” (a question your project plan flags you'll need to defend): The chain is still a perfectly legitimate Markov chain at every individual time step — at any given day, given you know the current state and you know whether today is an event-window day or not, the next state's probability is fully determined by a fixed matrix (either A_event-window or A_normal). You haven't broken the Markov property; you've just made the parameters of the chain depend on an observable, known-in-advance covariate (the event calendar), rather than insisting the same matrix has to apply unconditionally on all days. This is a standard, well-established extension in the time-series literature (sometimes called a Markov-switching model with time-varying transition probabilities, or TVTP-HMM) — it is not some ad hoc hack.2.5  What's “hidden” about a Hidden Markov Model?Everything in 2.1–2.4 describes a Markov chain where you can see the state directly. A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) adds one crucial twist: you cannot observe the state Sₜ directly. Instead, at each time step, the hidden state produces (or “emits”) an observable output, and you only get to see that output.In your project:Hidden state Sₜ = the market regime on day t (e.g., Regime 1 = calm/bull, Regime 2 = volatile/bear, Regime 3 = sideways). You never get to see this labeled directly anywhere — there's no government dataset that says “today was officially Regime 2.”Observed output Oₜ = the actual log return (and optionally volatility) on day t. This is directly visible — you literally compute it from price data.The HMM's job is to use the sequence of observed returns to make the best possible inference about which hidden regime produced each observation. This is exactly analogous to a classic textbook HMM example: imagine you're in a room with no windows and can't see the weather, but a friend outside tells you each day whether they carried an umbrella or not. You don't observe “rainy” or “sunny” directly (hidden state), but you observe “umbrella” or “no umbrella” (observation), and from a sequence of these observations you try to infer the most likely sequence of underlying weather. Your project replaces “weather” with “market regime” and “umbrella” with “today's return and volatility.”2.6  The emission distributionSince the hidden state produces an observable output, we need a rule for what outputs each state tends to produce. This is called the emission distribution (or observation model): P(Oₜ | Sₜ = i) — given you're in state i, what's the probability (density) of observing output Oₜ?Your project uses a Gaussian HMM: each hidden regime i is assumed to generate returns drawn from a Normal (Gaussian) distribution with its own mean μᵢ and standard deviation σᵢ:O_t | S_t = i   ~   Normal(mu_i, sigma_i^2)Concretely, this is what gives each regime a distinct “personality”:Regime “calm/bull”: small positive μ (e.g., +0.05% average daily return), small σ (e.g., 0.8% daily swing).Regime “crash/bear”: negative μ (e.g., −0.3%), large σ (e.g., 2.5% daily swing).Regime “sideways”: μ near zero, moderate σ.The model doesn't know these labels in advance — it just finds, statistically, the set of (μᵢ, σᵢ) pairs (one per state) that best explain the entire sequence of observed returns, plus the transition matrix A that best explains how states tend to follow one another. You then look at the fitted (μᵢ, σᵢ) values afterward and assign human-readable labels like “this looks like the crash regime” based on which historical dates got assigned to it (e.g., does it light up during COVID-2020?).2.7  The three classic HMM problems (and which ones your project needs)Every standard HMM textbook (Rabiner's tutorial, which your project explicitly tells you to read, is the classic reference) frames HMMs around three core computational problems. Knowing these names will make your reading and your eventual interview defense much easier:Evaluation problem: given a model (known A, known emission parameters) and an observed sequence, what's the probability that this model produced this sequence? Solved by the Forward algorithm.Decoding problem: given a model and an observed sequence, what is the single most likely sequence of hidden states that produced it? Solved by the Viterbi algorithm. This is what gives you your actual regime timeline (Day 2's “decode the most likely regime sequence”).Learning problem: given only the observed sequence (and not knowing A or the emission parameters in advance), how do you estimate the best-fitting A and emission parameters from data? Solved by the Baum-Welch algorithm, which is a specific instance of the more general EM (Expectation-Maximization) algorithm. This is how you “fit” the HMM to 5 years of NIFTY50 data in the first place.All three of these — Forward (and its sibling, Backward), Viterbi, and Baum-Welch/EM — are the algorithms your project's Day 4 derivation note must address, because your non-homogeneous twist (Part 2.4) changes how all three need to work, as we'll unpack fully in Part 3.Checkpoint — Part 2✓  Markov property: next state depends only on current state, not full history.✓  Transition matrix A: rows sum to 1, entry (i,j) = P(go from i to j).✓  Time-homogeneous = same A always; non-homogeneous = A can depend on a condition (your event-window indicator).✓  “Hidden” = you see the output (returns), not the state (regime) — you infer the state statistically.✓  Emission distribution = Gaussian per state, with its own mean/std — gives each regime a return “personality.”✓  Three classic problems: Forward (evaluate), Viterbi (decode), Baum-Welch/EM (learn/fit).Part 3 — The Core Math: Forward-Backward, Viterbi, Baum-Welch, and Your Non-Homogeneous ExtensionThis is the heaviest part of the guide — and the part your project explicitly says is “the part no off-the-shelf library or AI tool does for you.” Take it slowly. By the end you should be able to write the modified recursion on a whiteboard, as your project demands.3.0  Notation we'll use throughoutSymbolMeaningTTotal number of trading days in your sequence.NNumber of hidden regimes (states). Takes values in {1,...,N}.O_tObservation (log return, possibly with volatility) at time t.O_{1:T}The entire observed sequence O_1, O_2, ..., O_T.ATransition matrix, A[i][j] = P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i).pi_iInitial state probability, pi_i = P(S_1 = i) — probability the chain starts in state i on day 1.b_i(O_t)Emission probability/density, P(O_t | S_t = i). Once you have theta, you can decode the most likely regime sequence.3.1  The Forward algorithm (standard, homogeneous)Goal: compute P(O_{1:T} | theta) — the total probability of seeing this exact sequence of observations, given the model — without having to separately enumerate every possible hidden state sequence (which would be N^T possibilities — for 3 states and 1,250 trading days over 5 years, that's an astronomically large, computationally impossible number to brute-force).The trick: define a quantity called the forward variable:alpha_t(i) = P(O_1, O_2, ..., O_t, S_t = i | theta)In words: “the probability of having seen the observations up through day t, and landing in state i exactly on day t.” This is a clever quantity because it can be built up recursively — you compute it for day 1, then use day 1's values to compute day 2's, then day 2's to compute day 3's, and so on, all the way to day T. You never need to revisit earlier days once you've used them.Initialization (day 1):alpha_1(i) = pi_i * b_i(O_1)        for each state i = 1..NThis says: the probability of starting in state i AND immediately observing O_1, is just the prior probability of starting there times the chance that state would emit exactly what we saw.Recursion (day t, for t = 2 to T):alpha_t(j) = [ sum over all i of  alpha_{t-1}(i) * A[i][j] ]  *  b_j(O_t)In words, to land in state j on day t while having explained everything up to day t:You must have been in some state i on day t−1 (sum over all possible i).You transition from i to j using the matrix A — note: this is the only place the transition matrix A enters the formula.Once in state j, you must emit the observation actually seen on day t, captured by b_j(O_t).Termination:P(O_{1:T} | theta) = sum over all i of alpha_T(i)Sum across the final day's forward variables, over every possible ending state, and you get the total probability of the entire observed sequence.This recursion is O(N²T) instead of O(N^T) — for N=3, T=1250, that's about 11,250 simple operations instead of an impossibly large number. That's the entire reason the Forward algorithm exists: it's a dynamic-programming trick to avoid brute-force enumeration.3.2  The Backward algorithm (standard, homogeneous)Symmetric to Forward, but runs from the end of the sequence backward to the start. Define:beta_t(i) = P(O_{t+1}, O_{t+2}, ..., O_T | S_t = i, theta)“The probability of seeing all future observations from t+1 onward, given that we're in state i on day t.”Initialization (day T):beta_T(i) = 1     for all i(There's nothing after the last day, so this is trivially certain — probability 1.)Recursion (day t, going from T−1 down to 1):beta_t(i) = sum over all j of  A[i][j] * b_j(O_{t+1}) * beta_{t+1}(j)You don't strictly need Backward to do Viterbi decoding (3.3), but you absolutely need it for Baum-Welch / EM fitting (3.4) — it's what lets you compute, for every day, the probability distribution over states given the entire sequence (not just what came before, but also what came after). Combining Forward and Backward gives you the famous quantity:gamma_t(i) = P(S_t = i | O_{1:T}, theta) = [alpha_t(i) * beta_t(i)] / P(O_{1:T} | theta)gamma_t(i) is “the probability state i was the true regime on day t, given everything we observed across the whole 5 years” — using both past and future information. This is more informative than Viterbi's single best path, and it's the central quantity Baum-Welch uses to re-estimate parameters.3.3  The Viterbi algorithm (standard, homogeneous)Goal: find the single most likely entire sequence of hidden states S_1, ..., S_T that explains the observations — not just the most likely state on each individual day in isolation, but the best whole path taken together. (These can differ! The day-by-day-most-likely states don't necessarily form a coherent, individually-most-likely sequence, because transitions between them might be very improbable.)Define delta_t(i) = the probability of the single best path of length t that ends in state i on day t, while explaining O_1, ..., O_t:delta_t(i) = max over all state sequences S_1..S_{t-1} of  P(S_1,...,S_{t-1}, S_t=i, O_1,...,O_t | theta)Initialization:delta_1(i) = pi_i * b_i(O_1)psi_1(i) = 0   (no predecessor for day 1)Recursion (t = 2 to T):delta_t(j) = [ max over all i of  delta_{t-1}(i) * A[i][j] ]  *  b_j(O_t)psi_t(j)   = argmax over all i of  delta_{t-1}(i) * A[i][j]This looks almost identical to the Forward recursion — but notice the crucial difference: Forward sums over all i (because it wants total probability across all possible paths), while Viterbi takes the maximum over all i (because it only cares about the single best path). This sum-vs-max distinction is the entire difference between “evaluation” and “decoding.”psi_t(j) (“psi,” a bookkeeping array) records which previous state i was the best predecessor, so that after reaching the end, you can backtrack and reconstruct the full optimal path.Termination and backtrack:Best final state:  S_T* = argmax over i of delta_T(i)Then walk backward: S_t* = psi_{t+1}(S_{t+1}*)   for t = T-1 down to 1The resulting sequence S_1*, S_2*, ..., S_T* is your decoded regime timeline — this is literally what produces the “regime-colored bands” plot your Day 2 output asks for.3.4  Baum-Welch / EM (standard, homogeneous) — how you fit the HMM in the first placeSections 3.1–3.3 all assumed you already knew theta = {A, pi, mu, sigma}. But you don't — you have to estimate it from 5 years of data. This is the learning problem, solved by the Baum-Welch algorithm, a special case of the general EM (Expectation-Maximization) algorithm.The core difficulty: estimating theta would be easy if you knew the true hidden states (you could just compute averages/variances per state, and count transition frequencies). And decoding the true hidden states would be easy if you already knew theta (that's exactly what Viterbi/Forward-Backward do). But you have neither to start — it's a chicken-and-egg problem. EM solves chicken-and-egg problems with an iterative two-step loop:E-step (Expectation): Given the current (possibly initial-guess) parameters theta, run Forward-Backward to compute, for every day t and every state pair (i,j):gamma_t(i)    = P(S_t = i | O_{1:T}, theta)              [from 3.2]xi_t(i,j)     = P(S_t = i, S_{t+1} = j | O_{1:T}, theta)              = [alpha_t(i) * A[i][j] * b_j(O_{t+1}) * beta_{t+1}(j)] / P(O_{1:T} | theta)xi_t(i,j) (“the probability of being in state i at time t AND state j at time t+1, given the whole sequence”) is the new quantity here — it tells you, softly (as a probability, not a hard yes/no), how often each specific transition i → j seems to have happened across the dataset.M-step (Maximization): Use these soft, probability-weighted counts to produce updated parameter estimates that maximize the likelihood given those soft assignments:pi_i (new)      = gamma_1(i) A[i][j] (new)   = [ sum over t=1..T-1 of xi_t(i,j) ]  /  [ sum over t=1..T-1 of gamma_t(i) ]                = (expected number of transitions from i to j) / (expected number of times in state i) mu_i (new)      = [ sum over t=1..T of gamma_t(i) * O_t ]  /  [ sum over t=1..T of gamma_t(i) ]                = a gamma-weighted average of the observations, weighting each day by how                  likely it is to belong to state i sigma_i^2 (new) = [ sum over t=1..T of gamma_t(i) * (O_t - mu_i)^2 ]  /  [ sum over t=1..T of gamma_t(i) ]                = a gamma-weighted variance, same ideaYou then repeat: go back to the E-step with these new parameters, recompute gamma and xi, then M-step again, and so on, until the parameters stop changing much (convergence). Each full E+M cycle is mathematically guaranteed to never decrease the overall likelihood of the data — it's a “hill-climbing” procedure that reliably improves (or plateaus), though it can get stuck in a local optimum, which is why in practice you often try a few different random starting points.This is what hmmlearn's .fit() method does internally when you call it in Day 2 — it's running exactly this E-step/M-step loop for you, hidden inside the library.3.5  Now, the non-homogeneous modification — what actually changesYou now have everything needed to see precisely, line by line, what your project's core mathematical contribution is. Recall from Part 2.4: instead of one fixed A, you have two matrices, selected by a known, pre-computed indicator:E_t = 1  if day t is within +/-5 trading days of a scheduled corporate event (for the relevant stock)E_t = 0  otherwise A_t = A_event   if E_t = 1A_t = A_normal  if E_t = 0Critically: E_t is not a hidden quantity — you know it in advance for every day, because it comes straight from the (public, pre-announced) NSE event calendar. This is what makes the extension tractable: you're not asking the model to infer a third hidden thing; you're handing it a known, observed covariate and saying “use a different rulebook on these specific days.”Forward algorithm, modifiedGo back to Section 3.1's recursion:alpha_t(j) = [ sum over all i of  alpha_{t-1}(i) * A[i][j] ]  *  b_j(O_t)Every single A in every formula in Sections 3.1–3.4 simply becomes A_t (i.e., whichever of the two matrices is active for that specific transition into day t):alpha_t(j) = [ sum over all i of  alpha_{t-1}(i) * A_t[i][j] ]  *  b_j(O_t)That's it — that's the entire structural change to Forward. Nothing else moves. The reason this is valid (and not just a notational trick) is the same reasoning from Section 2.4: at each step, conditional on knowing E_t (which we always do, since it's observed data, not hidden), the transition still obeys the ordinary Markov/HMM recursion with a single, well-defined matrix. You've just stopped insisting it's the same matrix on every day.Backward algorithm, modifiedSame substitution, applied to the recursion that looks one step ahead:beta_t(i) = sum over all j of  A_{t+1}[i][j] * b_j(O_{t+1}) * beta_{t+1}(j)Note carefully: the transition being made is from day t to day t+1, so the matrix used is the one selected by E_{t+1} (the event-window status of the day you're transitioning into). Getting this off-by-one indexing right, consistently, throughout your implementation is one of the easiest places to introduce a subtle bug — be deliberate about whether “the event-window matrix applies to the day you're leaving or the day you're arriving at,” pick one convention, and use it everywhere.Viterbi, modifiedSame substitution again:delta_t(j) = [ max over all i of  delta_{t-1}(i) * A_t[i][j] ]  *  b_j(O_t)psi_t(j)   = argmax over all i of  delta_{t-1}(i) * A_t[i][j]Baum-Welch / EM, modified — this is the part requiring real careThe E-step formulas for gamma and xi change the same simple way (substitute A_t for A in the xi formula, since xi_t(i,j) involves the transition from day t to t+1):xi_t(i,j) = [alpha_t(i) * A_{t+1}[i][j] * b_j(O_{t+1}) * beta_{t+1}(j)] / P(O_{1:T} | theta)But the M-step update for the transition matrix changes more substantially, and this is the heart of your “from-scratch implementation” requirement. Instead of one pooled update using all T−1 transitions, you now compute two separate updates, each using only the subset of transitions that occurred under that regime of timing:A_event[i][j] (new)  = [ sum over t where E_{t+1}=1 of xi_t(i,j) ]  /  [ sum over t where E_{t+1}=1 of gamma_t(i) ] A_normal[i][j] (new) = [ sum over t where E_{t+1}=0 of xi_t(i,j) ]  /  [ sum over t where E_{t+1}=0 of gamma_t(i) ]In words: you take the same soft, probability-weighted transition counts (xi) and state-occupancy counts (gamma) you'd compute anyway, but now you split them into two buckets based on whether each transition happened during an event-window day or a normal day, and you re-estimate a separate transition matrix from each bucket. Emission parameters (mu_i, sigma_i) are not split this way in the simplest version of this model — the regimes' return/volatility personality is assumed to be the same wherever it occurs; only the probability of switching into/out of it is allowed to depend on event proximity. (You could in principle make emissions event-dependent too, but your plan correctly keeps that out of scope for two weeks — flag this as a design choice, not an oversight, if asked.)Why this can't just be done with hmmlearn out of the boxhmmlearn's GaussianHMM class is built around a single transmat_ attribute — internally, every Forward/Backward/Viterbi/Baum-Welch routine in that library hardcodes “use this one matrix for every transition.” There is no constructor argument or fitting option to say “use matrix A on these days and matrix B on those days” — that branching logic (the A_t selection based on E_t) does not exist anywhere in the library's internals. This is precisely the gap your project plan flags on Day 4: “identify exactly which hmmlearn internals you cannot reuse as-is.” You can (and should) use hmmlearn for the Day 2 baseline homogeneous model — that part is legitimately a solved, standard problem appropriate to use a library for. But the moment A needs to depend on E_t, you're outside what the library supports, which is exactly why Day 5 has you write the modified Forward-Backward and the modified M-step in raw numpy, by hand.The correctness check that proves your implementation works (Day 5)Your plan specifies a clean, elegant sanity test: if you artificially force A_event = A_normal (set them to be identical matrices), your non-homogeneous implementation should collapse back to behaving exactly like the ordinary homogeneous HMM — because if both “buckets” use the same matrix, the E_t indicator stops mattering at all, and the math reduces exactly to Sections 3.1–3.4. If your from-scratch numpy implementation, run this way, doesn't approximately reproduce hmmlearn's baseline output (same decoded states, same approximate log-likelihood), you have a bug. This single test is your strongest piece of evidence, in an interview, that your hand-derived math was implemented correctly — it's a real, checkable falsification test, not just “trust me.”3.6  Putting it all together: the full pipeline in one paragraphYou estimate two emission distributions per regime (mu_i, sigma_i for i=1,2,3) and two transition matrices (A_event, A_normal) using Baum-Welch/EM on 5 years of NIFTY50 returns, with E_t precomputed from the NSE event calendar. More states let the model fit the data more closely (lower error), but too many states risks overfitting — finding spurious, overly specific patterns that don't generalize, essentially “memorizing noise” rather than capturing real structure. You need a principled way to balance “fits the data well” against “isn't needlessly complicated.”BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) and AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) are both formulas that combine two things: (1) how well the model fits the data (technically, the log-likelihood — a number measuring how probable the observed data is under the fitted model, which Forward gives you directly, Section 3.1) and (2) a penalty for the number of parameters the model has (more states = more mu, sigma, and transition probabilities to estimate = more parameters):AIC = 2k - 2*log-likelihoodBIC = k*ln(T) - 2*log-likelihoodwhere k = number of free parameters in the model, T = number of data points. Both formulas reward a higher log-likelihood (better fit) but subtract a penalty that grows with model complexity — lower AIC/BIC is better. BIC penalizes complexity more heavily than AIC when you have a lot of data (because of the ln(T) term), which is why it's the more commonly used criterion for model-size selection in time series work, and why your plan specifically asks for BIC-justified state counts.In practice: you fit a 2-state HMM, compute its BIC; fit a 3-state HMM, compute its BIC; fit a 4-state HMM, compute its BIC; and pick whichever has the lowest BIC. Your plan explicitly tells you to write down the actual BIC numbers, not just assert “3 is standard” — this is exactly because “BIC said so, here are the three numbers” is a defensible, checkable claim, while “3 is standard” is not.4.7  What's a “ticker”?A ticker (or ticker symbol) is the short code used to identify a specific stock on an exchange — e.g., RELIANCE for Reliance Industries on NSE. Either is defensible, but you must pick one and be able to explain why — this is the kind of judgment call an interviewer will probe (“why did you choose per-stock vs. propagated?”). You then compare, for every stock, where the Day 2 baseline's Viterbi decode and your non-homogeneous Viterbi decode (Section 3.5) disagree about which regime was active, specifically in event windows — and save every such disagreement as a row: (stock, event_date, baseline_regime_call, new_model_regime_call). You need 80–100+ of these rows — this table is the bridge from Week 1's math into Week 2's financial judgment.Day 7 — Week 1 checkpoint & write-upA pure consolidation day: re-run everything end-to-end to confirm reproducibility (running the same code twice should give the same — or near-identical, accounting for any randomness in EM's starting point — results), and fix any data-quality gaps (e.g., a stock with too few events to be useful, or an event date that falls on a non-trading day and needs to be mapped to the nearest valid trading day) before you spend Week 2's limited time hand-verifying potentially-bad rows.Week 2 — Hand Verification, Validation, and the AppDays 8–9 — Manual verification (Section 1.6's filings, now read by you directly)This is the part of the project that is not math at all — it's you, personally, reading actual company disclosures (results PDFs, board meeting outcomes) for each of the 80–100 disagreement cases from Day 6, and making a financial judgment call: was the regime shift the new model detected actually backed by real, newly-disclosed information (e.g., a genuine earnings beat/miss, a guidance change, a governance issue), or was it not justified (e.g., a one-day price overreaction with no real new information, which would suggest the model is over-triggering near events just because volatility happens to be naturally a bit higher around them, not because anything meaningful actually happened)? You need a consistent rubric (a written-down, fixed definition of what counts as “justified” before you start labeling, not decided case-by-case as you go) so your labels are comparable to each other — and you explicitly check your own consistency by re-reading earlier labels partway through (Day 9), since judgment can subtly drift as you get faster or more tired. The instruction to flag cases where the baseline model was actually the better call is important scientific honesty: you are not trying to prove your new model is better, you're trying to find out whether it's better, and a dataset that only ever favors your own model is not trustworthy evidence to anyone, including yourself.Day 10 — Quantitative validationNow you use the labels from Days 8–9 as ground truth to ask: in the disagreement cases, how often was the event-conditioned model's call “justified” vs. how often was the baseline's call “justified”? This converts your subjective-but-rubric-disciplined judgment into a single comparable accuracy-like number for each model. Separately, you run the backtest from Section 1.8: a simple regime-gated long/avoid strategy, computed separately for event-window days vs. non-event days, and compute Sharpe ratio, max drawdown, win rate, compared against plain buy-and-hold. The honesty instruction repeats here for the same reason as Day 8–9: if the fancier event-conditioned model doesn't actually produce better risk-adjusted returns, that is a real, legitimate, reportable result (a model can be mathematically correct and well-validated while still not improving trading performance — these are different claims, and conflating them would be a misleading project).Day 11 — SHAP-style feature attribution & per-stock signal (Section 1.9, now in full)This layers a separate, more standard machine-learning model (XGBoost, a tree-based classifier — not related to the HMM math at all, just a different, complementary tool) on top of your regime output plus simple technical features (momentum = recent price trend strength, vol ratio = recent volatility relative to longer-term average, distance from event = your E_t-related feature again) to produce a buy/hold/avoid signal. SHAP then explains, for each individual prediction this classifier makes, how much each input feature pushed the prediction — and your hope/check is that the event-proximity feature visibly matters more for predictions made near scheduled events than far from them, which would be independent, complementary evidence (from a totally different modeling approach) supporting the same core hypothesis your HMM math was built around in Part 1.7.Day 12 — Streamlit app build (Section 4.5's tool, now used)You assemble everything into one interactive tool: type a ticker, see both models' current regime side by side, the next scheduled event with days-to-event, the Day 11 directional signal, and SHAP's top features — plus a price chart with regime bands (from Viterbi decoding, Section 3.3) and event-date markers. The “methodology panel stating honest performance numbers” instruction is a direct continuation of Day 10's honesty principle: the app must not oversell what Day 10 actually found.Day 13 — Deploy & polish (Section 4.5's deployment, now executed)You push the app live on Streamlit Community Cloud and clean the GitHub repo into the structure from Section 4.4, with a README whose opening section explicitly states the motivating gap — restating, for any outside reader, exactly what Part 1.7 and Part 2.4 established: standard HMMs assume constant transition probabilities, but the NSE event calendar gives you known, scheduled days where that assumption is suspect, and that's the gap this project closes.Day 14 — Final write-up & defense prepYour 1–2 page summary should be able to draw, almost verbatim, on this guide: the math (Part 3, in your own notation), the hand-verification methodology (Days 8–9), the honest quantitative results (Day 10), and two concrete worked examples (one where your new model was clearly right, one where it was wrong/ambiguous — picked from your actual Day 8–9 labeled data).
